Each message has 99 other members × 3 devices = 297 recipient devices, plus the sender's 2 other devices. That is **299 deliveries per message** and **29,900 device deliveries per second** at 100 messages per second.

At 1 KB = 1,000 bytes per body, the payload delivered to devices is **29,900,000 bytes/second = 29.9 MB/second = 239.2 Mbit/second**. This excludes transport/TLS headers, encryption overhead, retries, ACKs, and inter-service traffic. Sender ingress is separately 100,000 bytes/second. If a relay forwards ciphertext of a different size, use that size for actual network planning.

Storing the body once means 100 body writes/second and 100 KB/second of raw body growth, not 29,900 body copies/second. At sustained load this is 8.64 GB/day or 259.2 GB over 30 days, before replication, indexes, metadata, or compression.

Other writes depend on representation. A durable per-device delivery queue could create 29,900 pending-delivery records/second and a similar rate of acknowledgment updates. Per-user fanout could create 10,000 recipient references/second, including sender sync, with device cursors handling delivery independently. A shared group log could append 100 records/second and avoid those recipient copies, but still needs membership authorization and per-device progress tracking. Cursor ACKs can be cumulative and batched, so the network delivery count does not dictate the database write count. Replicas, indexes, retries, and deduplication add further work.

Deleting the server message after the first device ACK does **not** support a 30-day offline sync promise. That ACK proves receipt by one device only. Other devices may remain disconnected or fail before receipt. Retain a durable body/history record for at least the promised sync window, with per-device cursors and replay that tolerate duplicate delivery. ACKs may clear that device's pending entry without deleting shared history. Define the 30-day window precisely, including whether it is measured from message creation and how newly added devices receive history. If the server record is merely a transient routing entry and another authoritative copy retains the message for the full window, deleting that transient entry is safe; deleting the only retained copy is not.
